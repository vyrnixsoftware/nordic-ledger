## SCENE 1 | type=title | title="$331 Million to a Dictator's Daughter" | subtitle="Telia and Uzbekistan, audited"
Wednesday, 19 September 2012, Swedish public television. The investigative programme Uppdrag granskning broadcasts an episode titled, plainly, "TeliaSonera in Uzbekistan". Its finding: Sweden's former telephone monopoly, still more than a third owned by the Swedish state, had paid roughly two point two billion kronor, some three hundred and twenty million dollars, to a Gibraltar shell company for the right to operate in Uzbekistan. The shell had no office and no staff. Its registered owner was a twenty-four-year-old who had run a fashion boutique in Tashkent for Gulnara Karimova, the president's daughter. To understand how a Swedish state company got there, go back to 2007.

## SCENE 2 | type=quote | text="TeliaSonera has no insights into how Takilant has used the proceeds, or whether there are any connections to other persons in Uzbekistan." | attribution="TeliaSonera statement, September 2012"
That was the company's position on the night, and it is worth keeping. Not a denial that the money was paid: the payments were in the accounts. A statement that the company did not know where three hundred million dollars had gone once it left the building. Chief executive Lars Nyberg went further and staked his job on being cleared of bribery. He would be gone within five months. The Swedish state, which owned thirty-seven point three percent and nominated the chairman, said it had known nothing either. That is the recurring line.

## SCENE 3 | type=text | headline="Televerket to TeliaSonera" | bullets="Telia: Sweden's state telephone administration, Televerket, corporatised 1993 and listed 2000;2002: merger with Finland's Sonera, itself the former Finnish PTT;Swedish state 37.3 percent, Finnish state 11.7 percent, in 2012;Sonera brought Eurasian assets via Fintur: Kazakhstan, Azerbaijan, Georgia, Moldova"
The company itself. Telia was Televerket, the Swedish state telephone administration, turned into a company in 1993 and floated in 2000. In 2002 it merged with Sonera, Finland's equivalent, and inherited Sonera's holdings in the former Soviet south, through a Turkish-linked vehicle called Fintur. In 2012 the Swedish state still held thirty-seven point three percent, Finland eleven point seven, and headquarters sat at Stureplan in central Stockholm, a short walk from the finance ministry that owned it. Eurasia was, by then, the growth engine.

## SCENE 4 | type=map | region=auto | markers="Stockholm;Helsinki;Tashkent;Almaty;Baku;Tbilisi;Kathmandu;Gibraltar;Geneva;The Hague;New York;Washington"
The geography. Stockholm and Helsinki, the two former state monopolies. Tashkent, capital of Uzbekistan, thirty million people, a largely unconnected market and one of the most repressive states on earth. Almaty, Baku, Tbilisi, Kathmandu: the other Eurasian markets. Gibraltar, where Takilant was registered. Geneva, where the money was banked and later frozen. And two places with no role in 2007 and every role afterwards: The Hague, because Telia held its Uzbek assets through Dutch companies, and New York and Washington, because payments cleared in dollars and Telia's shares traded there. Nothing on this map is in Sweden except the head office.

## SCENE 5 | type=text | headline="Uzbekistan, 2007" | bullets="July 2007: TeliaSonera buys MCT Corp, owner of Uzbek operator Coscom, for about USD 300m;Karimova's associates control access to 3G frequencies and number series;December 2007: deal with Takilant Ltd of Gibraltar, owner Gayane Avakyan;Takilant gets 26 percent of Coscom's holding company for USD 30m, with a buyback right"
The entry. In July 2007 TeliaSonera bought MCT Corp, an American-registered company that owned Coscom, an Uzbek operator with a few hundred thousand subscribers and no 3G frequencies. Frequencies in Uzbekistan were, in practice, dispensed by people close to Gulnara Karimova, through her intermediary Bekhzod Akhmedov. In December 2007 TeliaSonera signed with Takilant, a Gibraltar company owned on paper by Gayane Avakyan, twenty-four, of Karimova's fashion house. Takilant would take twenty-six percent of Coscom's holding company for thirty million dollars, with the right to sell it back at a profit. The frequencies arrived. Board papers that year, American investigators later found, noted the "anti-corruption perspective". The deal went ahead.

What Takilant received, as later set out by the US Department of Justice. Thirty million dollars in 2007 for the shares that came with the 3G frequencies. Nine point two million in 2008 for number series and codes. Two hundred and twenty million in 2010, when TeliaSonera bought back the twenty-six percent it had sold three years earlier, at seven times the price. Then sham consulting contracts, a fifteen million dollar debt of a Swiss company owned by Karimova, and two million in cash to her representative. Total: about three hundred and thirty-one million dollars. A further payment was under discussion in late 2012, until Swedish television intervened.

## SCENE 7 | type=text | headline="Gulnara Karimova" | bullets="Eldest daughter of Islam Karimov, president of Uzbekistan 1991 to 2016;Diplomat, pop singer under the name Googoosha, fashion designer, businesswoman;2005 US cable: seen as a robber baron, the single most hated person in the country;Took payments from three foreign telecoms: about USD 865m in total, per US authorities"
The recipient. Gulnara Karimova was her father's ambassador in Madrid and Geneva, a jewellery and fashion designer, a pop singer under the name Googoosha, and, according to a 2005 American diplomatic cable released by WikiLeaks, viewed by Uzbeks as "a robber baron" and "the single most hated person in the country". She held no post in telecoms regulation and did not need one. Every foreign operator entering Uzbekistan dealt with her structure: TeliaSonera, Russia's MTS and the Dutch-registered VimpelCom. By the American reckoning she extracted around eight hundred and sixty-five million dollars from the three. TeliaSonera was the largest contributor, and the only one partly owned by a Nordic government.

## SCENE 8 | type=text | headline="Exposure, autumn 2012" | bullets="19 September: SVT's Uppdrag granskning, reporters Fredrik Laurin, Sven Bergman, Joachim Dyfvermark;Swiss prosecutors already investigating Takilant's Geneva accounts and Avakyan;26 September: Swedish prosecutor opens a bribery inquiry;October: Takilant funds frozen in Sweden, later about SEK 140m confiscated"
The reporters, Fredrik Laurin, Sven Bergman and Joachim Dyfvermark, had traced Takilant from Gibraltar to a Lombard Odier account in Geneva, where Swiss prosecutors had already opened a laundering case. A week after the broadcast the Swedish Prosecution Authority opened an inquiry into aggravated bribery, and in October a court froze Takilant funds held in Stockholm. Ucell, the Uzbek subsidiary, by then had nine million subscribers and was highly profitable. In Sweden the mood was closer to shock than outrage; the sum was unheard of in a Swedish bribery context. Sweden ranked fourth on Transparency International's index that year; Uzbekistan, a hundred and seventieth of a hundred and seventy-six.

The unwinding took three years and most of the top table. The board commissioned the law firm Mannheimer Swartling; its report landed on 1 February 2013 and the chief executive left that morning. The chairman went in April, replaced by Marie Ehrling. A new chief executive, Johan Dennelind, arrived in September and ordered a review by Norton Rose Fulbright of every Eurasian deal; four executives were dismissed in November. In Tashkent, Karimova fell out with her father and was confined to her house in February 2014; a month later Telia disclosed that the Americans and the Dutch were investigating. In September 2015, three years almost to the day after the broadcast, it said it would leave Eurasia entirely.

## SCENE 10 | type=quote | text="Crime could not be confirmed, but neither could it be dismissed." | attribution="Mannheimer Swartling review for the TeliaSonera board, 1 February 2013"
The lawyers' verdict, and note the construction. Not innocent, not guilty: unproven, both ways. The report said the company had failed to check who was behind Takilant, that its ethical guidelines had "certainly been violated", and that it had asked only whether payments were lawful, not whether they were wise. Nyberg said the review had cleared the company of bribery and resigned anyway. Chairman Anders Narvinger conceded it was "absolutely clear" the partner should have been vetted. Two months later he too was gone, at the request of the largest shareholder, the Swedish government.

## SCENE 11 | type=text | headline="What state ownership guaranteed" | bullets="Sweden's finance ministry held 37.3 percent and nominated the chairman and several directors;The state's ownership policy required exemplary conduct and annual sustainability reporting;Ministers said in 2012 they had no knowledge of the Uzbek deal;2013: the government demanded a new board, then took no further action against the company"
Here is the editorial question. A third of this company belonged to the Swedish taxpayer. The finance ministry's ownership unit sat on the nomination committee, chose the chairman and published a policy requiring state companies to be "role models". What did that guarantee? Judged by the record: a sustainability section in the annual report, a board that met, and a chairman who could be removed when the story broke. It did not guarantee that anyone in Stockholm asked who owned Takilant. State ownership was a promise about procedure, not outcome. When the outcome was three hundred million dollars to a president's daughter, the state's remedy was to change the directors and let foreign prosecutors do the rest.

## SCENE 12 | type=text | headline="Three telecoms, one official" | bullets="VimpelCom (Amsterdam-listed, Russian-controlled): USD 795m to US and Dutch authorities, February 2016;Telia: USD 965m, September 2017;MTS (Moscow): USD 850m to US authorities, March 2019;Combined: about USD 2.6bn in penalties for about USD 865m in bribes"
Telia was not alone. VimpelCom, Amsterdam-listed and Russian-controlled, went first, in February 2016: seven hundred and ninety-five million dollars to the United States and the Netherlands for a hundred and fourteen million in payments to Takilant, plus a compliance monitor. Telia followed in September 2017. MTS, the largest Russian operator, settled for eight hundred and fifty million in March 2019. Three companies, one recipient, two point six billion dollars in penalties, nearly all collected in Washington and The Hague. Telia's was then the third-largest foreign-bribery resolution in American history, behind Odebrecht and Siemens: three times the bribes paid, because the American method prices the profit the bribe bought, then adds a fine.

## SCENE 13 | type=text | headline="21 September 2017: the settlement" | bullets="Telia: three-year deferred prosecution agreement with the US Department of Justice, no monitor;Coscom, the Uzbek subsidiary: guilty plea to conspiracy to violate the FCPA;USD 274m criminal penalty to the US, USD 274m to the Dutch prosecutor, USD 457m disgorgement to the SEC;Up to USD 208.5m of the SEC amount reserved for a Swedish or Dutch forfeiture"
A year after the Americans and Dutch had proposed one point four billion, on 21 September 2017 the group signed a three-year deferred prosecution agreement with the Justice Department; the Uzbek subsidiary Coscom pleaded guilty in Manhattan. The criminal penalty was set at five hundred and forty-eight million dollars and split evenly with the Dutch, whose jurisdiction came from three Telia holding companies in the Netherlands. The SEC took four hundred and fifty-seven million in disgorgement, but agreed that up to two hundred and eight and a half million could be credited if Sweden, or the Netherlands, confiscated the profits themselves, within five hundred and forty days. That clause is the hinge of the second half of this story.

Follow the money to its destinations. The United States Treasury received about five hundred and twenty-three million dollars, once the credit is netted. The Dutch prosecution service received four hundred and eighty-two and a half million: its own two hundred and seventy-four, plus, in March 2019, the two hundred and eight and a half million reserved for Sweden. Sweden received nothing from the corporate settlement. Uzbekistan, whose citizens were on the wrong end of it, received nothing either. Sweden was in the courtroom the whole time. It just lost.

## SCENE 15 | type=text | headline="Stockholm District Court, 2018 to 2021" | bullets="Charged September 2017: Lars Nyberg, Tero Kivisaari, head of Eurasia, and Olli Tuohimaa, in-house lawyer;Prosecutor Gunnar Stetler also sought about SEK 1.4bn forfeiture from Telia;15 February 2019: all three acquitted, forfeiture claim rejected;4 February 2021: Svea Court of Appeal upholds the acquittals"
The day after the settlement, Swedish prosecutors charged three men with aggravated bribery: Nyberg; Tero Kivisaari, who had run Eurasia; and Olli Tuohimaa, the lawyer who papered the deals. On 15 February 2019, after a trial from September 2018, Stockholm District Court acquitted all three and threw out the forfeiture claim against Telia with them. The reasoning was not that no money had changed hands. It was that Swedish bribery law, as it stood before 2012, covered only payments to public officials, and Karimova, whatever she was, held no office in telecoms. The prosecution had proved a payment to a president's daughter and failed to prove a bribe. In February 2021 the Svea Court of Appeal agreed.

## SCENE 16 | type=quote | text="You don't pay three billion Swedish kronor to a businesswoman to help you get a telecoms licence." | attribution="Gunnar Stetler, chief prosecutor, closing argument, Stockholm District Court, 2018"
The prosecutor's summary of common sense, which two Swedish courts found was not the same thing as law. In Washington, the Foreign Corrupt Practices Act treats a president's daughter who controls access to licences as a foreign official by function; the same facts produced a guilty plea and a nine-figure penalty. In Stockholm, the statute of the day required a title, and the same facts produced three acquittals and no forfeiture. The law was amended in 2012, too late for 2007. So the country whose company paid the bribes could neither convict the men who approved them nor keep a krona of the proceeds. Not a scandal of judges. A scandal of drafting.

## SCENE 17 | type=text | headline="Leaving Eurasia, 2015 to 2020" | bullets="Ncell, Nepal: sold to Axiata for USD 1.03bn, 2016;Tcell, Tajikistan, 2016, Azercell and Geocell, 2018, Kcell, Kazakhstan, to Kazakhtelecom, December 2018;Ucell, Uzbekistan: sold to the Uzbek state for USD 215m, December 2018;Moldcell, Moldova, 2020: exit complete. Name changed to Telia Company, April 2016"
The retreat took five years. Nepal's Ncell went to Malaysia's Axiata for a billion dollars in 2016, the only clean profit in the exit. In 2018 Telia sold Azerbaijan, Georgia and, in December, its Kazakh operator Kcell to the state telecom. The same month it sold Ucell, the company at the centre of the affair, to the Uzbek government itself for two hundred and fifteen million dollars, a fraction of what it had paid to get in. Moldova, the last, went in 2020. In April 2016 the group dropped Sonera from its name and became Telia Company, a rebranding nobody pretended was unconnected, and moved headquarters from Stureplan to a business park in Solna. Fresh paint, new postcode.

## SCENE 18 | type=text | headline="Karimova, and the money" | bullets="House arrest 2014, secret sentence 2015, jailed 2019, a further 13 years and 4 months in March 2020;US indictment for money-laundering conspiracy, March 2019, still outstanding;Switzerland returned USD 131m in 2022 and USD 182m in 2025 via a UN trust fund; Belgium USD 108m in 2025;April 2026: Swiss criminal case against her discontinued after Tashkent refused to let her attend"
And the recipient. Confined to her house in 2014, secretly sentenced in 2015, jailed in 2019, and in March 2020 given a further thirteen years and four months for extortion and embezzlement, she remains in custody in Uzbekistan; her father died in 2016. American prosecutors indicted her in 2019 and cannot reach her. Her frozen fortune, estimated at up to one point four billion dollars, is trickling home: Switzerland has returned three hundred and thirteen million through a United Nations trust fund, Belgium a hundred and eight million. In April 2026 a Swiss court dismissed her trial because Uzbekistan would not let her leave to attend. The bank that held the account is still on trial.

## SCENE 19 | type=text | headline="Telia today" | bullets="Swedish state stake risen to about 41 percent through buybacks; Finland sold out in 2018;Chief executive Patrik Hofbauer since February 2024, the fifth since Nyberg;Denmark sold to Norlys 2024, Latvian stakes being sold to the Latvian state, TV4 and MTV to Schibsted;A Nordic and Baltic operator again, with a Swedish state that still says nothing"
Telia in 2026 is a smaller, quieter company. Denmark was sold in 2024, the Latvian stakes are going to Riga, the television business to Schibsted, and some three thousand jobs were cut. Patrik Hofbauer has run it since February 2024, the fifth chief executive since Nyberg. Because of share buybacks, the Swedish state's holding has drifted up to around forty-one percent, larger than at any point during the scandal. Ask the ownership unit what changed after Uzbekistan and you will get a document about sustainable business. Ask what the state recovered and the answer is shorter.

## SCENE 20 | type=text | headline="Our view: the flag was not a control" | bullets="A state shareholding gave Telia a state chairman, a state policy and a state's presumption of decency: none of them checked Takilant;The exposure came from a public broadcaster, not the owner, the auditor or the regulator;Sweden's law could not convict and its court would not confiscate: the money went to Washington and The Hague;Karimova is in prison, three executives are free, and Uzbekistan is being repaid by Switzerland"
Our position. The Telia affair is usually told as a story about a company that lost its way abroad. We think it is a story about ownership. Sweden's stake gave the company a state chairman, a state ethics policy and, most valuable of all, the presumption that a Swedish state company does not do this. That presumption was extended to a deal that a twenty-minute search would have punctured. The exposure came from the state's own broadcaster; the penalty from foreign prosecutors; the recovery to Uzbekistan from Swiss courts. Sweden charged, lost, and received nothing. Ownership is not oversight. That is not a moral judgement. It is an accounting one.
