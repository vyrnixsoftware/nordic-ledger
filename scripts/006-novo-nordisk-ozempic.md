## SCENE 1 | type=title | title="Bigger than Denmark" | subtitle="Novo Nordisk, Ozempic and Europe's most valuable company"
Friday, 1 September 2023. At the close in Copenhagen, Novo Nordisk, maker of Ozempic and Wegovy, is worth four hundred and twenty-four point seven billion dollars. LVMH, Bernard Arnault's luxury conglomerate, is worth four hundred and twenty point one. For the first time, Europe's most valuable company is a Danish diabetes firm headquartered in Bagsværd, a suburb best known for a rowing lake. Its market value is now larger than the entire annual output of Denmark. Nine months later it will peak near six hundred and forty billion. Two years after that it will have lost two-thirds of it, sacked its chief executive, replaced its board and cut nine thousand jobs. This is how a country came to depend on a molecule.

## SCENE 2 | type=quote | text="We see a direct link between the growing role of the pharmaceutical industry in the Danish economy and upwards pressure on the currency." | attribution="Danske Bank, August 2023"
That is a Danish bank explaining, with a straight face, that Denmark's central bank was holding interest rates below the European Central Bank's partly because of a diabetes drug. It was true. Denmark pegs the krone to the euro, and dollars earned in America by Novo Nordisk were flowing home fast enough to push the currency up. The Nationalbank's answer was to raise rates less than Frankfurt did. Most countries acquire that kind of problem from oil. Denmark acquired it from an injection pen. To see how, we need to go back a century, to a Nobel laureate returning from Canada with a licence in his luggage.

## SCENE 3 | type=text | headline="1923: two laboratories and one licence" | bullets="August Krogh, Nobel physiologist, visits Toronto in 1922 and secures rights to make insulin;Nordisk Insulinlaboratorium founded 1923 with diabetologist H.C. Hagedorn;1925: the Pedersen brothers, ex-Nordisk, set up rival Novo Terapeutisk Laboratorium;Both firms adopt foundation ownership from the 1920s onward"
In 1922 August Krogh, a Copenhagen physiologist with a Nobel Prize, went to Toronto, where insulin had just been isolated, and came back with permission to make it in Scandinavia. His wife Marie had diabetes; that concentrated the mind. With the physician Hans Christian Hagedorn he founded Nordisk Insulinlaboratorium in 1923. Two years later two brothers, Harald and Thorvald Pedersen, left after a falling-out and started Novo Terapeutisk Laboratorium across town. For sixty-four years Nordisk and Novo competed, sued and improved each other's insulin, which is the polite Danish way of saying they hated one another. Both, unusually, were placed under charitable foundations, which decided that profits belonged to science rather than to heirs.

## SCENE 4 | type=text | headline="1989: the merger, and who owns it" | bullets="Novo and Nordisk merge in 1989 into Novo Nordisk A/S, HQ Bagsværd;Novo Nordisk Foundation, via Novo Holdings, holds ~28% of capital and ~77% of votes;A shares carry ten votes each, are unlisted and by statute can never be sold;Foundation assets DKK 1,114bn (~$165bn) at end-2023: the world's largest charitable foundation by assets;Grants: over DKK 10bn in 2024"
In 1989 the rivals merged into Novo Nordisk, and their foundations merged into one. The Novo Nordisk Foundation, through Novo Holdings, today owns about twenty-eight percent of the shares and seventy-seven percent of the votes, because its A shares carry ten votes each, have never been traded, and under the articles cannot be. No hedge fund can buy this company; no board can be voted out by outsiders. At the end of 2023 the foundation's assets stood at over eleven hundred billion kroner, some hundred and sixty-five billion dollars, more than Gates and Wellcome combined. It gives away ten billion kroner a year, and funds roughly a quarter of Danish medical research. Hold that structure in mind. It is about to matter.

Semaglutide was a diabetes drug first. Ozempic, a once-weekly injection, reached the American market at the end of 2017; the same molecule at a higher dose, sold as Wegovy for weight loss, followed in 2021, and promptly ran out. Demand went beyond anything the Kalundborg plant on the Danish coast could fill, and beyond the company's own forecasts. In August 2023 the SELECT trial showed a twenty percent cut in heart attacks and strokes, which turned a cosmetic drug into a medical one, and its shares rose seventeen percent in a month. By June 2024 the company was worth around six hundred and forty billion dollars. In three years the world's most valuable European firm had gone from luxury handbags to appetite suppression.

The shape of the story in one line. Roughly a hundred and thirty billion dollars at the end of 2019, a solid but unremarkable insulin company. Two hundred and fifty at the end of 2021 as Wegovy launched. Four hundred and sixty at the end of 2023. A peak near six hundred and forty in June 2024, when the company was worth more than the Danish economy produces in a year, and more than the next several Copenhagen-listed firms combined. Then down: three hundred and ninety by December 2024, roughly two hundred and twenty a year later, and around two hundred billion this August. Two-thirds of the value gone in twenty-six months. Every institution in Denmark that had grown attached to it, and there were many, went along for the ride.

## SCENE 7 | type=text | headline="What one company does to a country of six million" | bullets="Corporate income tax paid: about $2.3bn in 2023;Roughly half of Danish exports growth in 2023 traced to pharma, mostly Novo;Danish pension funds and the ATP hold large positions: Novo dominates the Copenhagen index;Kalundborg: DKK 42bn plant expansion, a town of 16,000 nicknamed 'Novo Town';Bagsværd HQ, plus Hillerød, Måløv, Gentofte, and Chapel Hill in the US"
Consider the arithmetic of a country of six million people. In 2023 Novo Nordisk paid around two point three billion dollars in corporate income tax. Its exports, booked mostly as goods made abroad but owned in Denmark, drove the country's trade surplus. Danish pension funds, including the state-backed ATP, held it heavily because it dominated the Copenhagen index. In Kalundborg, a coastal town of sixteen thousand, the company was spending forty-two billion kroner on new plants, and locals had begun calling the place Novo Town. Skilled workers, permitting officers and property prices were pulled toward it. Economists began using a phrase that had last been heard in Helsinki around 2011.

Danmarks Statistik has published a number that most statistics offices never need to compute: growth with and without the pharmaceutical industry. In 2023 the Danish economy grew about one percent; without pharma it shrank. In 2024 it grew three point nine percent, of which two point three points came from pharma alone. Only in 2025, on the June 2026 revision, did the rest of the economy contribute more to growth than the drug industry, for the first time since 2021. Between 2019 and 2025 Danish value added rose sixteen point six percent; strip out pharma and it is eight. Denmark, in other words, spent three years growing at Nordic pace outside Bagsværd and at Chinese pace inside it.

## SCENE 9 | type=text | headline="Bagsværd sets the interest rate" | bullets="Krone pegged to the euro since 1999: rates follow the ECB;2023: Nationalbank raises rates less than the ECB, keeping the spread at minus 40 basis points, to lean against krone strength;No currency intervention since December 2022;March 2026: spread still minus 40bp, krone gradually weakening"
Denmark has pegged its currency to the euro since the euro existed, which means its central bank normally copies Frankfurt and goes home. In 2023 it stopped copying exactly. As Ozempic dollars flowed in and the krone pressed against the strong side of its band, the Nationalbank raised rates by less than the ECB, leaving Danish policy rates forty basis points below the euro area's, and it has held that gap ever since. Danish mortgage holders, in effect, received a small subsidy from American appetite. The Nationalbank has not needed to intervene in the market since December 2022, and by 2026 the krone was gently weakening, which tells you something about the direction of the drug money too.

## SCENE 10 | type=quote | text="If Novo continues to be most of the growth in the Danish economy, then there's a problem when the profit stream from Novo shrinks. And it will shrink." | attribution="Economist quoted by Fortune, May 2024, on Denmark's 'Nokia risk'"
The comparison everyone reached for was Finland. We told that story in an earlier video: at its peak, Nokia was about four percent of Finnish GDP and most of the Helsinki stock exchange, and when it lost the smartphone, Finland lost a decade. Denmark in 2024 was, on paper, more exposed than Finland had been. There is one difference, and it cuts both ways. Nokia was owned by everyone and answered to no one. Novo Nordisk is owned by a foundation that cannot be bought out and does not have to sell. That is a very good structure for surviving a bad decade. It is a less obvious structure for avoiding one.

## SCENE 11 | type=text | headline="2024: the year the competition arrived" | bullets="Eli Lilly's Mounjaro and Zepbound (tirzepatide) take US share month by month;US telehealth and compounding pharmacies sell copycat semaglutide at a fraction of the price;20 December 2024: CagriSema trial shows 22.7% weight loss vs the 25% promised: shares fall ~20% in a day;Lilly's revenue grows 32% in 2024, Novo's 26%"
The problem was not that Wegovy stopped selling. It was that a better version arrived. Eli Lilly's tirzepatide, sold as Mounjaro and Zepbound, produced more weight loss in head-to-head trials and was taking American prescriptions month by month. Beneath both, a legal grey market of compounding pharmacies, allowed while Wegovy was officially in shortage, sold copycat semaglutide by mail order for a fraction of the list price. Novo's answer was a successor, CagriSema. On 20 December 2024 the trial reported twenty-two point seven percent weight loss against the twenty-five the company had promised, and the shares dropped a fifth in a day. That is the day the wobble began.

## SCENE 12 | type=text | headline="16 May 2025: the foundation moves" | bullets="CEO Lars Fruergaard Jørgensen, eight years in the job, to step down;Reason given: 'market challenges' and the share price fall since mid-2024;Statement notes the Foundation 'initiated dialogue' with the board;Foundation chair Lars Rebien Sørensen, ex-CEO, joins the board as observer;Shares already down ~50% from peak"
On 16 May 2025 the company announced that Lars Fruergaard Jørgensen, chief executive for eight years, the man who had presided over the entire rise, would step down. The stated reason was the share price. The statement also contained a sentence that governance specialists read twice: the foundation had "initiated dialogue with the board regarding accelerated succession". Its chairman, Lars Rebien Sørensen, who had run Novo Nordisk himself for sixteen years, would join the board as an observer. Nothing in this was hostile, and nothing in it was optional. Seventy-seven percent of the votes had spoken, and the board had listened. Jørgensen described his time as a privilege. He stayed on through the summer.

## SCENE 13 | type=text | headline="29 July 2025: the profit warning" | bullets="Sales growth outlook cut from 13-21% to 8-14%: profit outlook cut to 10-16%;Reason: 'persistent use of compounded GLP-1s' in the US, slower market growth;Shares fall over 20% in one session, about $70bn of value;Maziar Mike Doustdar, 33-year company veteran, head of international operations, named CEO from 7 August;Shares down ~37% year to date, before he starts"
On 29 July 2025 the company cut its outlook for the year and named its next chief executive in the same release, which is not the order most companies would choose. Sales growth guidance went from thirteen to twenty-one percent down to eight to fourteen. The reason given was continued use of compounded semaglutide in America, months after the FDA had ordered compounders to stop, and slower growth in the market itself. The shares fell more than a fifth in a session, roughly seventy billion dollars. Maziar Mike Doustdar, an Austrian-Iranian who had joined the company in Vienna in 1992 and ran everything outside America, took over on 7 August. His inheritance was a business still growing at double digits and a share price that had halved.

## SCENE 14 | type=text | headline="10 September 2025: nine thousand" | bullets="9,000 jobs cut, ~11% of a 78,400 workforce; 5,000 of them in Denmark;Target: DKK 8bn (~$1.3bn) of annual savings by end-2026;2025 operating profit outlook cut again, to 4-10% growth;Restructuring costs of DKK 8bn booked in Q3;Kalundborg and Bagsværd brace: the country's largest private employer shrinks"
Five weeks in, Doustdar cut nine thousand jobs, about eleven percent of a workforce of seventy-eight thousand, five thousand of them in Denmark. The company that had been vacuuming up Danish engineers was now returning them. Savings were to reach eight billion kroner a year by the end of 2026, and, in the same breath, the profit outlook was cut again. "Sometimes the hardest decisions are the right ones," he said, which is what chief executives say. What was different was the setting. In most countries a drugmaker cutting a tenth of its staff is a business story. In Denmark it was a macroeconomic event, discussed by the finance ministry, and in Kalundborg, where the plants were still going up, it was the weather.

## SCENE 15 | type=text | headline="Autumn 2025: the board goes, and the price comes down" | bullets="21 October: chairman Helge Lund and six independent directors resign after a rift with the Foundation over pace of change;14 November: EGM elects Lars Rebien Sørensen chairman for 'two to three years';6 November: deal with the Trump administration: Wegovy $349 a month via TrumpRx, ~$245 for Medicare, pill from $149;Insulin capped at $35: 60 to 80% cuts from list prices"
In October the foundation finished the job. Chairman Helge Lund and six independent directors said they would go, after what Lund called, with Nordic understatement, a difference of view with the majority shareholder over the pace of change. On 14 November an extraordinary meeting elected Lars Rebien Sørensen chairman, for a stated two to three years. Between those dates, on 6 November, Novo and Lilly stood in the Oval Office and agreed to cut American prices: Wegovy at three hundred and forty-nine dollars a month direct to consumers, around two hundred and forty-five for Medicare, a starter pill at one hundred and forty-nine. In exchange, Medicare would cover obesity drugs, and tariff threats faded. Volume for price. Every forecast in Bagsværd was rewritten again.

## SCENE 16 | type=text | headline="Lilly overtakes" | bullets="Tirzepatide (Mounjaro + Zepbound) sales 2025: $36.5bn, the world's best-selling drug;Lilly holds more than 60% of US GLP-1 prescriptions by late 2025;Lilly 2026 revenue guidance: $80-83bn, up ~25%;Novo 2025 sales: DKK 309bn (~$50bn), up 10%: Ozempic ~DKK 127bn, Wegovy DKK 79bn;Novo 2026 guidance: sales down 5 to 13%: first decline in modern history"
By the end of 2025 the ranking had flipped. Lilly's tirzepatide sold thirty-six and a half billion dollars, overtaking Merck's Keytruda as the best-selling medicine on earth, and Lilly held more than sixty percent of American GLP-1 prescriptions. Novo's 2025 was, by any ordinary standard, fine: three hundred and nine billion kroner of sales, about fifty billion dollars, up ten percent, with Ozempic alone at a hundred and twenty-seven billion. The difference was direction. On 4 February 2026 Lilly guided to eighty billion dollars of revenue, up a quarter. The same morning, Novo guided to a fall of five to thirteen percent, its first decline in living memory, and its shares dropped eighteen percent in Copenhagen. Two top executives left in the same release.

Read as a list, twenty months of it. Every entry but the last two is a subtraction. Some were self-inflicted, such as the CagriSema targets, and some, such as the American price deal, were done to Novo and to Lilly alike. In between, in January 2026, came the one clear win: an oral Wegovy at a hundred and forty-nine dollars a month that put a hundred and seventy thousand Americans on treatment in four weeks. Then in February the head-to-head trial confirmed that CagriSema, the successor molecule, lost to Zepbound. And on 31 July 2026 a heart-failure drug called ziltivekimab failed its main trial, and thirty billion dollars left in a day. This is what a company looks like when it has one great product and the market keeps asking for the second.

## SCENE 18 | type=text | headline="Mid-2026: where it stands" | bullets="4 August 2026: Q2 sales DKK 78.5bn, up 7%; guidance raised to 0 to -6%;US injectable Wegovy sales down 22% on price; pill has ~90% of the US oral obesity market, 5m prescriptions;Headcount down ~12,000, about 15%, in a year;Shares still down about two-thirds from June 2024; market value ~$200bn;Novo Nordisk Foundation assets fell from DKK 1,060bn to DKK 694bn in 2025 alone"
Which brings us to now. On 4 August 2026 the company reported second-quarter sales of seventy-eight and a half billion kroner, up seven percent, and raised its guidance for the year to somewhere between flat and minus six, which counts as good news when the previous range began at minus four. Injectable Wegovy in America is down twenty-two percent on price; the pill has nine-tenths of the oral market and is adding patients in Britain faster than anywhere. Headcount is down twelve thousand. The shares, having recovered thirty percent from a March low, sit about two-thirds below the peak, and the company is worth roughly two hundred billion dollars, behind ASML, SAP, LVMH and half a dozen others. And the foundation that owns it watched its assets fall by three hundred and sixty-six billion kroner in a single year.

## SCENE 19 | type=quote | text="Because of the setback, we need to have more shots at goal. We need to be faster and we need to learn from the failures and reinvent ourselves again." | attribution="Mike Doustdar, CEO, 5 August 2026"
That is the current chief executive, a year in, promising acquisitions and a September investor day. Note what he did not say. He did not say the market had shrunk; it is still expected to pass a hundred billion dollars a year by 2030. He did not say the drug had stopped working. Semaglutide still brings in more than thirty billion dollars a year, and the patents in America hold until about 2031. What he described was a company that had one shot and needs several. Nokia's engineers said something similar in 2010. The difference is that Doustdar's shareholder cannot be bought, cannot sell, and has already shown it will change the manager rather than the strategy.

## SCENE 20 | type=text | headline="Our view: strength, or the Nokia trap?" | bullets="Strength: foundation ownership means no forced sale, no activist, patient capital, and a board that can be replaced in a month;Weakness: the same structure concentrates a nation's tax, pensions and growth on one owner's judgement;Denmark grew 2.4% ex-pharma in 2025, better than the euro area: it is not yet Finland;The trap is not Novo failing; it is a country planning as if it can't;Test to watch: what Danish growth, tax and the krone do in the 2031 patent cliff"
Our position. Denmark's Novo dependence has so far been a strength that looked like a risk, and it is becoming a risk that still looks like a strength. The foundation model did what its founders intended: when things went wrong, capital did not flee, no one was taken over, and the owner replaced the chief executive and the board within six months, which the average Anglo-Saxon share register could not manage in three years. But that same permanence concentrates a nation's tax base, its pensions and its growth statistics on one owner's judgement about one molecule. Denmark is not Finland in 2012. The rest of its economy grew in 2025 without pharma, and Danish public finances entered this with a surplus. The Nokia trap is not that the champion fails. It is that a country plans as though it cannot. Denmark has been given a warning at a bearable price. Watch what it does before 2031.
