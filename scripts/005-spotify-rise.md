## SCENE 1 | type=title | title="300 Million Subscribers, 18 Years of Losses" | subtitle="The Spotify ledger, audited"
Tuesday, 3 April 2018. Outside the New York Stock Exchange, staff hoist a flag to welcome the day's new listing: a white cross on a red field. Switzerland. The company is Swedish. It is corrected within minutes, and the exchange later tweets that it was "a momentary ode to our neutral role in the process of price discovery." The company was Spotify. It had hired no underwriter, would ring no bell and was raising no money. Its shares opened at one hundred and sixty-five dollars ninety. To understand why a Swedish music company that had never made an annual profit was worth thirty billion dollars that morning, go back to Stockholm.

## SCENE 2 | type=quote | text="Normally, companies ring bells. Normally, companies spend their day doing interviews on the trading floor touting why their stock is a good investment." | attribution="Daniel Ek, blog post, 2 April 2018"
The founder's note the evening before. Spotify's direct listing had no bankers setting a price, no roadshow and no new shares; existing holders simply sold to whoever turned up. The exchange published a reference price of one hundred and thirty-two dollars. The stock opened twenty-five percent above it, drifted, and closed at one hundred and forty-nine dollars and one cent, a market value of about twenty-six and a half billion dollars. Ek had, he wrote, been running an unusual company for twelve years and saw no reason to change on the day it went public.

## SCENE 3 | type=text | headline="Sweden, 1998 to 2006: the wiring" | bullets="1998: Hem-PC scheme lets employees lease tax-subsidised home computers, roughly a million machines in a decade;Early 2000s: municipal fibre and Bredbandsbolaget give Sweden some of the fastest, cheapest home broadband anywhere;2003: The Pirate Bay founded in Stockholm by the anti-copyright group Piratbyrån;2006: Swedish police raid The Pirate Bay's servers, the site is back within three days"
Streaming came from Sweden for reasons. In 1998 the government let employers lease home computers to staff tax-free, and about a million households took one. Municipal fibre then sold ten-megabit connections while the rest of Europe queued for dial-up. Fast pipes and idle teenagers produce predictable results. By 2003 Stockholm hosted The Pirate Bay, the largest torrent tracker in the world, run by people who regarded copyright as a foreign import. When police raided its servers in May 2006, it was back online within three days. Sweden had run the world's biggest experiment in free music. Somebody was going to sell the answer.

## SCENE 4 | type=map | region=auto | markers="Stockholm;Helsinki;Oslo;London;Paris;Madrid;New York;Luxembourg;Munich;Skellefteå"
The geography. Stockholm, where the company was founded and its engineers still sit. Helsinki, Oslo, London, Paris and Madrid: with Sweden, the six markets in the October 2008 launch, where the licences could be had. New York, for the July 2011 American launch and, seven years later, the listing. Luxembourg, where Spotify Technology S.A. is legally domiciled. And two later detours: Munich, home of the drone maker Helsing, and Skellefteå, home of the battery maker Northvolt, both recipients of Daniel Ek's money once he had some.

## SCENE 5 | type=text | headline="Ek and Lorentzon, 2006" | bullets="Daniel Ek: born 1983 in Rågsved, a southern Stockholm suburb, coding jobs from his teens, sold ad firm Advertigo to TradeDoubler in 2006, briefly chief executive of uTorrent;Martin Lorentzon: co-founder of TradeDoubler, listed 2005, sold shares and put in the first money;Spotify AB registered April 2006, first prototype in Ek's flat in Rågsved;The name: a misheard word, later back-explained as spot plus identify"
The founders. Daniel Ek grew up in Rågsved, a concrete suburb south of Stockholm whose previous contribution to Swedish music was the punk band Ebba Grön, who would not have approved of any of this. He built websites at fourteen, sold a small advertising firm to TradeDoubler in 2006 and, for a few months, ran uTorrent, the file-sharing client. TradeDoubler's co-founder Martin Lorentzon supplied the capital. They registered Spotify AB in April 2006 and built the prototype in Ek's flat. The name came from a word Lorentzon misheard across the room; the tidier explanation was invented afterwards. Ek was twenty-three.

## SCENE 6 | type=quote | text="I realised that you can never legislate away from piracy. Laws can definitely help, but it doesn't take away the problem. The only way to solve the problem was to create a service that was better than piracy and at the same time compensates the music industry." | attribution="Daniel Ek, interview with The Daily Telegraph, February 2010"
The thesis, and it has not changed. Pirated music was free but slow, virus-ridden and mislabelled. A legal catalogue that started playing in under half a second, with a search box, would beat free. Ek had run uTorrent; he knew the competition. The novelty was not the technology so much as the deal: instead of suing Swedish teenagers, pay the labels a share of whatever the teenagers could be induced to spend. The labels, in 2006, thought that was nothing. That is what the next two years were about.

## SCENE 7 | type=text | headline="Two years for the licences, and IPRED" | bullets="2006 to 2008: Ek and Lorentzon negotiate with the four majors and the independents' agency Merlin;October 2008: labels take 18 percent of Spotify for a total of EUR 8,804, plus advances and a revenue share;7 October 2008: launch by invitation in six countries, free with advertising or SEK 99 a month;1 April 2009: Sweden's IPRED law lets rights holders demand file-sharers' identities, Swedish internet traffic falls about 30 percent overnight;17 April 2009: The Pirate Bay's four founders sentenced to a year in prison and SEK 30m damages"
It took two years. Universal, Sony, Warner and EMI, plus Merlin for the independents, agreed terms in 2008: advances, a share of revenue and, in a detail the Swedish press unearthed a year later, eighteen percent of the company for eight thousand eight hundred and four euros. Spotify launched on 7 October 2008, free with adverts or ninety-nine kronor a month. Then the Swedish state did it a favour. On 1 April 2009 the IPRED law came into force, letting rights holders unmask file-sharers, and traffic through Stockholm's internet exchange fell by roughly a third in a day. A fortnight later a Stockholm court sent The Pirate Bay's founders to prison. Piracy became inconvenient. Spotify was standing right there.

Twelve years from prototype to public company, and every step involved a rights holder saying no first. Sean Parker, of Napster fame, wrote Ek a fan letter in 2009 and invested through Founders Fund in 2010. Taylor Swift pulled her albums in 2014 over the free tier and returned in 2017. Apple, which had once sold the industry its digital future for ninety-nine cents a track, launched a rival in June 2015 with no free tier and a great deal of cash. Spotify's answer was more subscribers, sooner, at almost any cost.

## SCENE 9 | type=text | headline="America, 14 July 2011" | bullets="Two years of negotiation, launched with a free tier capped by hours, USD 4.99 unlimited, USD 9.99 premium with mobile;Warner Music signed the day before launch;About 10 million users and 1.6 million payers in Europe at the time;Ek spent much of 2010 and 2011 in New York closing the deals"
The American launch, on 14 July 2011, was two years late, because American labels were the most suspicious of free. Warner signed the day before. The compromise was the model that still stands: a free tier that nudges you towards paying, and a ten-dollar subscription with mobile and offline listening. This was the freemium bet, and it was genuinely a bet. Every free listener cost money in royalties. The wager was that enough would convert, and that the rest were the marketing budget. It worked, in the sense that revenue climbed vertically for a decade. Profit is another matter.

The growth curve, without a bad year on it. Twenty-eight million paying subscribers at the end of 2015, ninety-six million at the listing, two hundred and ninety million at the end of 2025 and, on 4 August 2026, three hundred million, with seven hundred and seventy-seven million people using the service every month. No music company in history has had a customer list of that size. Every one of them listens to a catalogue that Spotify rents rather than owns, and the rent is a percentage of the takings.

## SCENE 11 | type=text | headline="The 70 percent" | bullets="Roughly 70 percent of Spotify's revenue goes to rights holders: labels, distributors, publishers and collecting societies;Gross margin stuck between 25 and 27 percent from 2018 to 2023, against 60 to 80 percent for most software firms;Payouts to the industry: over USD 60bn cumulative by end 2024, USD 11bn in 2025 alone;Spotify does not set the wholesale price of its only product: three major labels do"
The arithmetic that governs everything else. Roughly seventy cents of every dollar Spotify collects goes straight out again to whoever owns the recording and the song. That was the price of the licences in 2008 and it has barely moved, because Universal, Sony and Warner control about two-thirds of the music people actually want. So Spotify's gross margin sat at about a quarter of revenue for its first years as a public company, a figure that would embarrass a supermarket. The Swedish way of beating the pirates was to out-service them, and then spend fifteen years paying the labels for the privilege.

The consequence. Revenue rose from under two billion euros in 2015 to fifteen point seven billion in 2024, and in every one of those years but the last, Spotify lost money. Two thousand and seventeen was the worst, at one point two billion euros, partly a paper charge on convertible notes. Two thousand and twenty-one came within thirty-four million of break-even, and then the company went shopping for podcasts. Then, in 2024, one point one billion euros in a single year. What changed was not the seventy percent. It was everything around it.

## SCENE 13 | type=text | headline="3 April 2018: the direct listing" | bullets="No underwriters, no roadshow, no new shares, no lock-up: existing holders sold into the open market;Reference price USD 132, opened USD 165.90, closed USD 149.01, market value about USD 26.5bn;The labels' 2008 stakes, bought for EUR 8,804, worth about USD 2.6bn on the day, and Sony sold half its holding within weeks;Ek and Lorentzon kept control through Swedish-style beneficiary certificates with extra votes"
Back to the flag. The direct listing was a Swedish sort of decision: why pay Wall Street seven percent to price a company that had traded privately for years? It cost tens of millions in fees instead of hundreds, and Slack, Coinbase and others copied it. The labels did rather well too. The shares they had bought for eight thousand euros a decade earlier were worth around two and a half billion dollars by the close, and Sony cashed in half its stake almost immediately, promising to pass a share to artists. The pirates' nemesis had made the record companies richer than the pirates ever made anyone.

## SCENE 14 | type=text | headline="What the artists say" | bullets="Payout per stream commonly quoted at USD 0.003 to 0.005, though Spotify pays rights holders, not artists, and does not pay per stream;Taylor Swift out 2014 to 2017, Neil Young out January 2022 over Joe Rogan and back in 2024;2024: tracks under 1,000 annual streams stop earning royalties;Loud and Clear 2026: 13,800 artists earned over USD 100,000 in 2025, about 1,500 over USD 1m"
The other side of the seventy percent is who receives it, and it is mostly not musicians. Spotify pays labels and publishers in proportion to streams; what reaches the artist depends on a contract Spotify never sees. Hence the perennial per-stream figure, a fraction of a cent, true and meaningless, and hence the protests: Taylor Swift in 2014, Neil Young in 2022, and in 2024 a rule that songs under a thousand streams a year earn nothing. Spotify's answer is an annual report called Loud and Clear: eleven billion dollars paid out in 2025, thirteen thousand eight hundred artists earning over a hundred thousand. Both are describing the same pipe from opposite ends.

## SCENE 15 | type=text | headline="The podcast detour, 2019 to 2023" | bullets="2019: Gimlet, Anchor and Parcast bought for about USD 400m combined, then The Ringer and Megaphone in 2020;May 2020: Joe Rogan exclusive, reported at USD 100m and later at more than double that, plus deals with the Obamas and the Sussexes;Total spend on podcasting widely put at over USD 1bn;2023: three rounds of lay-offs, 600 in January, 200 in June, 1,500 in December, about a quarter of staff in total"
Near break-even, Spotify decided the way past the seventy percent was to own content instead of renting it. Podcasts carry no label royalty. So from 2019 it bought studios, Gimlet, Anchor, Parcast, The Ringer, and talent, most famously Joe Rogan, for a sum first reported at a hundred million dollars, later at well over two hundred. Rogan brought a vaccine-misinformation row and Neil Young's departure; the Sussexes made one series; the Obamas left. Exclusive shows lost most of their audiences. Over a billion dollars later, Spotify shut studios and, in 2023, dismissed about a quarter of its staff. The bet was not on podcasts as such. It was on escaping the labels. It did not.

## SCENE 16 | type=quote | text="In hindsight, I was too ambitious in investing ahead of our revenue growth." | attribution="Daniel Ek, letter to staff announcing 1,500 redundancies, 4 December 2023"
An unusually plain sentence for a chief executive. Spotify had hired through the pandemic assuming cheap money and podcast growth would continue; neither did. The December 2023 cut, seventeen percent of the workforce, followed six percent in January and the podcast unit in June. Investors, who had watched the shares fall from about three hundred and sixty dollars in early 2021 to under seventy-five in late 2022, took the apology as a promise. What followed was the least Swedish period in the company's history: price rises, cost discipline and margin.

## SCENE 17 | type=text | headline="2024: the first profitable year" | bullets="2024: revenue EUR 15.67bn, operating income EUR 1.37bn, net income EUR 1.14bn, the first full-year profit since 2006;Gross margin 30.1 percent, up from about 25 percent, on price rises, cheaper podcasts and marketplace fees to labels;2025: operating income about EUR 2.2bn, gross margin 33.1 percent, 290 million subscribers, 751 million monthly users;Q2 2026: revenue EUR 4.78bn, gross margin 33.4 percent, net income EUR 545m, 7,302 employees"
The turn came in 2024. Revenue of fifteen point seven billion euros, operating profit of one point four billion, net profit of one point one billion: the first full year in the black since the company was registered. Two things did it. Prices went up in dozens of markets and hardly anyone left. And the gross margin finally moved, to thirty percent, then thirty-three, not because the labels took less but because Spotify sold them promotional tools, wrote off the podcast excess and stopped hiring. Two thousand and twenty-five roughly doubled the operating profit. The seventy percent still leaves; there is simply more of the other thirty.

A Spotify subscription in America cost nine dollars ninety-nine for twelve years, which, allowing for inflation, means it got cheaper every year while the catalogue grew. Then three rises in under three years: ten ninety-nine in 2023, eleven ninety-nine in 2024, twelve ninety-nine from February 2026, with family and duo plans up more. Each was announced with the same sentence about reflecting value, and each was followed by more subscribers. The freemium bet had a second half: once you have converted a good part of the world's music listeners, you can raise the rent.

## SCENE 19 | type=text | headline="Ek's side bets: Northvolt and Helsing" | bullets="2020: Ek pledges EUR 1bn of his fortune to European deep-tech moonshots via Prima Materia;September 2020: backs Northvolt's USD 600m round, Northvolt files for bankruptcy in Sweden in March 2025;2021: Prima Materia leads Helsing's first round of about EUR 100m, Ek becomes chairman;June 2025: Prima Materia leads a EUR 600m round valuing Helsing at EUR 12bn, and a run of artists pull music from Spotify in protest"
The founder's own ledger. In 2020 Ek promised a billion euros of his fortune to European technology "moonshots" through a vehicle called Prima Materia. His first was Northvolt, the Skellefteå battery maker whose collapse we covered in our first episode; that money is gone. His second was Helsing, a Munich defence software and drone company, where he is chairman and which he led to a twelve-billion-euro valuation in June 2025. Musicians who had tolerated fractions of a cent drew the line at drones: Deerhoof, Massive Attack and others removed their music. Neither bet has anything to do with streaming, which is presumably the point.

## SCENE 20 | type=text | headline="Spotify, 2026" | bullets="1 January 2026: Ek becomes executive chairman, responsible for capital allocation and long-term direction;Co-chief executives Gustav Söderström, product and technology, and Alex Norström, business, both long-serving co-presidents;4 August 2026: 300 million subscribers, 777 million monthly users, quarterly revenue EUR 4.78bn, operating income EUR 655m;Guidance: 305 million subscribers and EUR 5bn revenue for Q3 2026"
On 30 September 2025 Ek announced he would step back, and on 1 January 2026 he did, becoming executive chairman while Gustav Söderström and Alex Norström, who had run product and business respectively for years, became co-chief executives. It is a European arrangement, closer to a Swedish industrial family than to Silicon Valley, and Ek said he had handed over most of the running some time before. In August 2026 the company reported three hundred million subscribers, a record gross margin and quarterly revenue approaching five billion euros. Twenty years after the prototype in Rågsved, it is the largest music retailer on earth and still owns no songs.

## SCENE 21 | type=text | headline="Our view: they out-served the pirates and rented the shop" | bullets="Sweden's broadband, its home-computer subsidy and The Pirate Bay made the country the natural laboratory for legal streaming;Spotify won by being more convenient than free, and by paying the labels for it;The price was structural: about 70 percent of revenue out the door, and no annual profit until 2024;It is now a very large, genuinely profitable company with margins a software investor would still call thin"
Our position. Spotify is a genuine European success, and often misread. It did not disrupt the record industry; it saved it, on terms the industry wrote. Ek's insight was that convenience beats free, and that the way to license the world's music was to give its owners most of the money and a slice of the company. That worked spectacularly for the labels, well for listeners, unevenly for musicians and, for eighteen years, not at all for Spotify's accounts. The 2024 profit came from higher prices and lower costs, not a better deal. Sweden beat the pirates by out-serving them. It then spent fifteen years paying the labels for the privilege, and it still does.
